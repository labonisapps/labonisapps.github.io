Indexonis

Indexonis reads a photograph of a selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern taken on a transmission electron microscope and identifies the crystal. You can index a pattern in front of the instrument, or later when you look back at your data.

Every intermediate result is shown on screen and can be corrected. Nothing is decided behind your back.

Not on Google Play yet. This page describes the app as it stands; the store link will appear here when it is published.

Step by step

1Start from a photograph — or from the microscope file itself

Load a pattern you photographed off the screen of the microscope. A shot taken at an angle is straightened first, so you do not have to line the camera up perfectly.

Native microscope files (.dm3, .dm4, .emd) open directly. There is also FFT analysis from lattice fringes, and a diffraction simulator you can drive from the CIF list. 56,000+ structures are bundled and work offline.

2The scale bar is read for you

0.20 1/A, 2.0 1/nm, 5 nm^-1 and the other notations instruments use are all handled. The camera constant comes from the number, the unit and the bar length, so nothing has to be typed in.

The screen shows the cropped region it read, the same region with the recognised text drawn over it, the detected bar length in pixels, and the resulting value. If the bar was hard to read, type the number in yourself.

3Pick what to match against

The CIF list searches by name or formula, or by picking elements on the periodic table. Structures you come back to can be starred to keep them at the top.

Tapping a CIF opens its detail screen, which is also the way in to the diffraction simulation.

4Or search the open database

When the structure is not on hand, search the Crystallography Open Database by material name or formula and pull the CIF down. This is the only part of the app that uses the network.

5Simulate the pattern you expect to see

Give a structure and a zone axis as u v w, and the app draws the pattern it should produce. Camera length, spot size, thickness, text size and rotation are all adjustable, and you can switch between kinematical and dynamical (two-beam) intensities.

Forbidden reflections are marked, so an extinction rule that has been broken is visible rather than silently ignored.

6Read off the candidates, with the numbers behind them

Candidates come back sorted by match score, each with the indexed reflections drawn over your own pattern and a table of theoretical against measured d-spacings.

Absolute matching (real distances, from the scale bar) and ratio matching (distance ratios and angles only) are shown together. The app does not pick one for you. When the two disagree, that is your hint that the scale bar was read wrongly.

Good to know

  • The pattern centre is estimated from the inversion symmetry of the reflections, then refined by a Gaussian fit on the transmitted beam. If no inversion centre is found it falls back to the largest bright blob, and the app tells you which of the two answered, because they can disagree
  • Spots are fitted to sub-pixel accuracy
  • The ratio result states, as a percentage, how far the camera constant it implies sits from your calibration value

Your data

Everything is calculated on the device. The app has no account, collects no personal information, and sends nothing to us. Searching the Crystallography Open Database is the only time it reaches the network, and that request carries only your search words.
